Ask any small business owner how their week went and you'll usually hear some version of "busy." Ask them where the time went, and things get fuzzier. Some of it went to customers. Some went to the work they actually love. And a surprising amount went to things that just... happened. Reconciling a spreadsheet. Chasing an invoice. Answering the same question for the third time that week.

That last bucket is where AI earns its keep. But you can't fix what you can't see, so before talking about tools, agents, or anything else, start here: a quick audit of where your week goes.

It takes about fifteen minutes. All you need is a notepad or a blank spreadsheet and an honest memory of the last five working days.

## Step 1: List the recurring tasks

Think back over the past week and write down every task that you or your team did more than once. Don't filter yet. Small things count. Especially small things.

If you're stuck, walk through a typical day:

- **Morning:** What's the first thing you check? Email, orders, the schedule, yesterday's sales?
- **Money:** Who sends invoices, follows up on late payments, pays suppliers, matches receipts?
- **Stock and supplies:** Who counts, reorders, and deals with what's missing?
- **People:** Scheduling, time off requests, onboarding, payroll questions?
- **Paperwork:** Permits, licenses, insurance, tax prep, anything with a deadline?
- **Customers:** Quotes, follow-ups, reviews, the same five questions on repeat?

Most owners end up with fifteen to thirty items. That's normal.

## Step 2: Put a number on each one

Next to each task, write down roughly how much time it takes in a typical week, across everyone who does it. Round generously. If you aren't sure, guess, then check your gut: does it *feel* bigger or smaller than that?

Then add two quick ratings, from 1 to 3:

- **Repetitive:** Is it the same steps every time? (3 = nearly identical every time)
- **Painful:** How much does everyone dread it, or how costly is it when it goes wrong? (3 = "I hate this" or "this has burned us before")

Your sheet will look something like this:

| Task | Hours/week | Repetitive (1-3) | Painful (1-3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sending and chasing invoices | 4 | 3 | 3 |
| Answering "are you open on…" calls | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| Weekly inventory count | 5 | 2 | 3 |
| Building next week's schedule | 3 | 2 | 2 |
| Meeting with key customers | 6 | 1 | 1 |

## Step 3: Find your best candidates

Now look for the tasks that score high on all three: lots of hours, very repetitive, and painful. Those are your best candidates for AI.

A quick way to sort them is to multiply hours by repetitive by painful and sort by the result. In the example above, invoicing (4 × 3 × 3 = 36) and the inventory count (5 × 2 × 3 = 30) jump right to the top. The customer meetings land at the bottom, which is exactly where they belong. That's the work you *want* to be doing.

A few patterns tend to show up again and again:

- **Anything that moves information from one place to another**, like copying orders into a spreadsheet or invoices into your accounting software.
- **Anything that follows up**, like payment reminders, lead follow-ups, or review replies.
- **Anything with a deadline you track in your head**, like renewals, filings, and contract dates.
- **Anything that answers the same question repeatedly**, whether that's from customers or from your own staff.

## Step 4: Pick one

It's tempting to try to fix everything at once. Don't. Pick the single task at the top of your list and focus there first.

One well-chosen improvement does two things. It gives you back real hours right away, and it shows your team what's possible without asking them to change everything about how they work. The second and third improvements get a lot easier after that.

## What to do with your list

At this point you have something most businesses never make: a clear picture of where your time goes, and a short list of the places it's leaking.

Sometimes the fix is an existing tool you can set up in an afternoon. Sometimes it's a custom AI agent that connects the systems you already use. And sometimes it's simply a process change that doesn't need technology at all. The audit helps you tell the difference.
