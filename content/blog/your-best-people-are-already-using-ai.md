Someone I know was recently let go for using AI at work.

They hadn't done anything reckless or malicious. They had taken a handful of slow, manual processes, the kind that used to take weeks, and used AI to get them down to minutes. That's the kind of improvement most businesses dream about.

Then the company's security team reviewed what they'd built and found a problem: in one step, a credential was being handled in a way it shouldn't have been. A real issue, and worth fixing. But instead of fixing it, the company shut everything down and let my friend go.

The thing is, though, the company had never told anyone how they were allowed to use AI. No policy, no approved tools, no "check with us before you connect anything to our systems." Nothing.

## People are going to use AI either way

AI makes work easier, and it can make people's lives easier, too. So your most capable people are going to use it, with or without permission. The more initiative someone has, the more likely they are to be the first to try.

Without clear guidance, mistakes are almost inevitable. Usually that's because nobody told people where the lines are. Most people have never had to think about where an access key should live, or what happens to data they paste into a chatbot.

## Was there a real problem? Yes

I'm not trying to paint anyone as the bad guy, and the security team were just doing their job. After all, security concerns about AI are legitimate. Credentials, customer data, and financial information all deserve care, and a shortcut that exposes any of them can cause real damage.

Should my friend have looped in the security team before rolling out what they built? Probably, sure. Most of us would agree with that in hindsight.

But there's a big difference between "this needs to be fixed" and "this person needs to go." The issue they found could have been corrected in an afternoon. The improvement it was attached to was worth far more than that. The company threw out the baby with the bathwater, and the work my friend did went with them.

## Your competitors are paying attention

The story doesn't end there. My friend is now interviewing, and the offers coming in are substantially higher than what they were making before. Other employers see someone who turned weeks of work into minutes, and they recognize exactly what that's worth.

That's the real risk for a business that responds to AI with fear. Your best people won't stop using it. They'll just go somewhere that values it. And they'll take their skills, and their momentum, with them.

## What a simple AI policy covers

You don't need a 40-page document. A small business can cover the essentials on a page or two:

- **Approved tools.** Which AI tools people can use for work, and which accounts to use them with.
- **What never goes in.** Passwords, access keys, and other credentials. Sensitive customer information. Anything you'd be uncomfortable seeing on the front page of the news.
- **Handling keys and credentials.** If an AI tool needs access to your systems, how that access is set up, stored, and limited, and who signs off on it.
- **When to ask first.** A simple rule like "anything that connects to customer data or company systems gets a second set of eyes before it goes live."
- **Checking the output.** AI makes mistakes, so a person reviews anything that goes to a customer or affects money. Our post on [trusting AI](/blog/how-do-you-trust-ai) goes deeper on how to do that well.
- **What happens when something goes wrong.** Mistakes get reported, fixed, and turned into a better rule. Nobody gets punished for raising a problem early.

If you do nothing else, have a plan for that last one. Mistakes happen and if people are afraid of being fired for making one, they'll hide their AI use instead of asking for help. That's how small problems become big ones.

## When something does go wrong, respond in proportion

Even with a policy in place, someone will eventually make a mistake. When it happens:

1. **Pause the specific thing that's broken**, not everything the person has built.
2. **Fix the issue together.** The person who built it understands it best and can usually fix it fastest.
3. **Update the policy** so the next person doesn't make the same mistake.
4. **Recognize the win.** If someone saved the business weeks of work, say so, even while you're fixing the problem.

Handled this way, a security scare becomes a lesson for the whole team, and the person who took the initiative becomes your go-to expert instead of your next resignation letter.

## Turn your AI power users into champions

The people already experimenting with AI are an asset. Give them a clear set of rules, a person to check with, and a way to share what they've learned. They'll help everyone else get better at it, and they'll have every reason to stay.
